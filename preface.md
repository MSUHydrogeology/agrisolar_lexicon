# Preface: What this document is for

There is not yet an agrisolar design community. There are solar developers, agronomists, conservation biologists, hydrologists, extension agents, county planners, and farmers who each hold part of the problem and have no common vocabulary for the whole of it. A shared language is not a convenience for such a community — it is a precondition for one. Until the words are settled, practitioners cannot tell whether they actually disagree or are merely using terms differently, and the field burns its energy on definitional friction instead of design.

This lexicon is therefore descriptive, not prescriptive. It aims to let two people who have never met describe their sites to each other precisely, recognize which design problems they share, and locate their genuine disagreements.

**Three structural devices carry the whole document:**

1. **The Scale Transect** — a single gradient, from farmstead to regional, that most other terms are read against. Scale determines what is physically, economically, and institutionally possible before design begins.
2. **The Sharing–Sparing Axis** — whether the array shares land with production or spares land by taking low-value ground out of it. Conceptually independent of scale, though not fully free of it (see §2.2).
3. **Design Pressures** — the competing forces every site must settle. This is where the document's normative energy lives, deliberately, because pressures are supposed to have direction.

Four figures carry the argument: the transect (Fig. 1), the mode shift across it (Fig. 2), the feasible region formed with the sharing–sparing axis (Fig. 3), and the pressures themselves (Fig. 4). Source SVGs are in `figures/` and may be reused independently.

The four design templates in circulation are not the object of this lexicon. They are **settlements** — four ways the pressures have been resolved, at particular scales and sharing postures. Naming the forces is what lets a fifth, sixth, and twentieth settlement be described in the same language.

### What we learned from *The Lexicon of New Urbanism*

That document is not a dictionary. It is the terminological layer of a regulatory stack that escalates: **Master Plan** (illustrative, voluntary) → **Guidelines** (enforceable by covenant) → **Code** (enforceable by contract) → **Ordinance** (municipal law). The lexicon supplies vocabulary that survives the climb — a term means the same thing at the sketch stage and the statute stage. That continuity is the actual product.

Four transferable lessons:

**The transect is the load-bearing device, and it came from ecology.** The rural-to-urban Transect organizing the SmartCode traces to Humboldt's geographic cross-sections, used to analyze natural ecologies across shore, wetland, plain, and upland. Duany borrowed it for human settlement. Applying transect logic to agroenergy landscapes returns the device to its origin discipline.

**Codes must be calibrated, and not every zone appears everywhere.** Transect-based codes require local calibration, and a given place may have more or fewer zones than the model — most towns have no urban core at all. Our scale zones work the same way: a region with no transmission-adjacent land simply has no A5, and the lexicon must not imply a deficiency.

**Modularity drove adoption.** The SmartCode spread as open-source freeware with pluggable modules contributed by outside experts. Our pressures and levers should be separable, so a hydrologist can extend one without touching the others.

**Rigidity is the documented failure mode.** Miami 21 became rigid enough that developers sought exceptions to build affordable housing; Poundbury is visually coherent and socially narrow. Critics charge form-based codes with producing uniformity and privileging appearance over function. The agroenergy analog is acute: a lexicon that hardens into a scorecard could lock in ground-cover prescriptions and foreclose the experimentation this field still needs. **Describe and compare; resist prescribing.**
